Almost every guide to church short-form content skips this part, and it's the part that ends media ministries.

Not copyright takedowns, usually. What ends them is a conversation that goes: a clip goes out, someone in it didn't expect to be in it, a leader sees something they'd have wanted to approve, and suddenly the whole thing is a governance question being handled by people who are upset. The volunteer who made the clip feels blamed. They stop.

All of that is preventable with about two hours of work upfront and a single page of writing.

*This is practical guidance from working through the problem, not legal advice. For anything with real exposure — a dispute, a takedown, a policy going into your bylaws — talk to an attorney who handles nonprofit or media law.*

## The four permissions

There are four separate things you need permission for, and churches tend to think about one of them.

### 1. The preacher

Start here. Not as a formality — as a real conversation, because the dynamics are genuinely different from a livestream.

A sermon in a room is delivered to people who know the speaker, know the church, and heard the preceding thirty minutes. A clip is delivered to strangers with none of that. The same sentence can be pastoral in the room and inflammatory in a feed.

What to actually ask:

- Are you comfortable with clips going out from every sermon, or do you want to approve each one?
- Are there topics where you'd rather nothing be clipped? (Most preachers have two or three — often anything touching a specific family's situation, and often anything political.)
- If you get emotional, is that in or out?
- Who can say "take that down," and does it come down immediately without a discussion?

Get the answers written down. Not because you don't trust each other, but because in eighteen months a different volunteer will be doing this job and they weren't in the room for this conversation.

Most pastors say yes to broad permission with a standing right to pull anything. That's a good arrangement — it lets the team move fast without a bottleneck, while keeping the veto where it belongs.

### 2. Anyone else who appears

This is where churches get sloppy and where the actual hurt happens.

**Congregation members in the background.** Wide shots of the room end up on the internet. Most people don't mind. Some people mind very much — people in recovery, people fleeing an abusive situation, people whose immigration status makes them cautious, people who simply haven't told their family they've started attending church.

The standard practice is a posted notice at every entrance: this service is recorded and may be shared publicly, speak to someone at the welcome desk if you'd prefer not to be filmed. Then actually maintain a "please don't film" area, and actually tell your camera operators where it is.

**Anyone giving a testimony.** Get this in writing every time, without exception. Testimonies are the highest-performing content churches produce and also the most sensitive — they're frequently about addiction, abuse, infidelity, or mental health.

A short written release covering: what it's for, that it may be posted publicly and shared beyond the church, that it can be deleted on request, and their signature. One page. Do it before you record, not after.

And honor the deletion request without negotiating, even when the clip is doing well. Especially then.

**Children.** Different and stricter. Written parental consent, specific to media use, and most churches sensibly avoid identifying kids by name in public posts at all. If your church has a child protection policy, media should be in it — and if it isn't, this is a good reason to update it.

### 3. Music

This is where the actual copyright risk sits, and it's routinely misunderstood.

The general shape of it: streaming licenses like CCLI's are designed to cover **worship services streamed by your church**. That is a specific scope. Cutting a piece of worship footage out and posting it as a standalone clip on TikTok is a different use, and you should not assume it's covered just because your Sunday stream is.

Check your actual license terms. CCLI publishes what its licenses cover and their support team will answer direct questions about social media use — ask them specifically rather than reasoning by analogy from your streaming license.

Separately from the licensing question, there's an enforcement question, and it bites regardless: **automated copyright detection on Meta and YouTube will flag recognizable recorded music**, and it doesn't check your license first. Repeated flags can restrict or damage your account's standing, which is a slow disaster for a page you've spent two years building.

The practical guidance almost every church lands on:

- **Clip the spoken word.** Sermons, scripture, testimony, prayer. No licensing complexity at all — your church owns it.
- **Avoid clipping worship music**, especially contemporary songs still in copyright.
- **Watch the boundaries of your sermon clips.** If a clip starts three seconds before the band comes back in, that's enough for detection to catch it. Trim tight.
- **If music is genuinely central to your church's identity and you want to post it**, confirm your coverage in writing before you build a strategy on it.

There's a real cost to this — worship footage is often the most beautiful thing your church produces. But sermon content outperforms it in short-form anyway, for reasons that have nothing to do with licensing. Covered in [20 sermon moments worth clipping](/blog/sermon-clip-ideas).

### 4. Anything quoted from someone else

Preachers quote. Books, films, other preachers, news clips. Short spoken quotes attributed properly are generally fine and normal. Playing a substantial chunk of a film or another preacher's video is a different matter and shouldn't be clipped and reposted.

If your pastor's illustration is built around someone else's video playing on the screens, that clip is not yours to post.

## The one-page media policy

This is the artifact that makes everything above survive turnover. Write it once. Keep it to one page — a ten-page policy is a policy nobody reads.

What it needs to say:

1. **What gets recorded.** Which services, which cameras, what happens to the file.
2. **Who approves what.** Standing approval for sermon clips; named person who reviews anything sensitive; the pastor's standing veto.
3. **Who can post.** Named roles, not names — "the media coordinator," not "Jessica," because Jessica will move.
4. **Consent requirements.** Notice posted at entrances; written release for testimony; parental consent for minors.
5. **The music rule.** Your church's actual decision, in one sentence.
6. **Takedown procedure.** Who to contact, and a commitment to remove on request without requiring a justification. Put a real email address in it.
7. **Where accounts and passwords live.** Genuinely important and almost always forgotten. Churches lose access to their own Instagram accounts constantly because the only login was a volunteer's personal email and that volunteer moved to Arizona in 2023.

Take it to whoever governs your church — elders, deacons, board. Get it approved formally. It takes one meeting, and afterward your media team is operating with authority rather than assuming permission.

## The conversation to have first

If you're a volunteer who wants to start clipping and none of this exists yet, don't write a policy and present it. Start with a fifteen-minute conversation with your pastor:

> "I'd like to start cutting short clips from your sermons for Instagram and Facebook. Before I do, I want to know what you're comfortable with and get a couple of things written down so nobody's guessing later. Can I get fifteen minutes?"

That request has never once been received badly. What gets received badly is a clip appearing online that nobody was asked about.
