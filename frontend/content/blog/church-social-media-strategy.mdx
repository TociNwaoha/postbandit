Open the Instagram account of almost any church and you'll find the same feed: a graphic with service times, a photo from the youth retreat, a verse on a gradient background, an announcement about the potluck.

There's nothing wrong with any of it. It just isn't reaching anyone who doesn't already attend, which is usually the stated reason the account exists.

The gap is structural. Church social media is mostly built as a **bulletin board** — a place to inform people who are already in — while the goal is described as **outreach**. Those require completely different content, and running the first while hoping for the second is why so many church accounts feel like they aren't working. They're working fine. They're just doing a different job than the one leadership thinks they're doing.

## Decide which account you're running

Before anything else, pick one:

**The bulletin board.** Service times, events, registration links, weather cancellations, photos from Sunday. Audience is your existing congregation. Success is measured by whether people showed up to the thing you announced. This is legitimate and useful — most churches need it.

**The reach account.** Content aimed at people who have no relationship with your church. Success is measured by whether people outside your building watched, saved, and shared.

You can run both, but not from the same posting strategy, and if you try to blend them you'll get an account that under-serves both audiences.

If you're going to run one, the honest recommendation for most churches: **use Stories and email for bulletin-board content, and make the main feed a reach account.** Stories are where your existing people already look, they expire, and nobody judges them. The feed is your front door.

## The platforms, honestly

**Facebook.** Still the most important platform for most churches in the US, and the one that everyone under 35 keeps writing off. Your 45+ congregation is there, your local community groups are there, and Facebook remains the strongest platform for actually getting people to show up to a physical event in a specific town. Facebook Pages also support scheduled and automated posting, which matters when you're running a volunteer team.

**Instagram.** Where you reach 20–40s. Reels carry essentially all of the discovery — a Reel can reach far outside your following, while a grid post reaches your existing followers and stops. If you post one thing to Instagram, make it a Reel.

**YouTube.** Two separate jobs. Long-form is your sermon archive and it's how people vet a church before visiting — a genuinely underrated function. Shorts is discovery, and it's the platform where sermon clips have the longest tail; a Short can still be picking up views eight months later, which is not true anywhere else.

**TikTok.** Highest ceiling for reaching people with no church background at all, and the most volatile. Worth doing if you have someone who genuinely understands the platform's tone. Not worth forcing.

**Threads and X.** Text-first, low production cost. Good for quotes and one-liners. Small returns for most local churches, but nearly free if you're already producing the content.

**The realistic answer for a volunteer team:** Facebook and Instagram, seriously, plus YouTube for the archive. Add anything else only after those are consistent for three months.

## What to actually post

Roughly, in order of how well it performs for churches:

**1. Sermon clips.** This should be the backbone. You already produce the source material every week, it's emotionally substantive, and it's evergreen. Most churches treat their sermon as a one-time broadcast rather than the content library it is. The full breakdown of what to cut is in [20 sermon moments worth clipping](/blog/sermon-clip-ideas).

**2. Testimony.** A person telling their own story. This outperforms almost everything, and almost no church does it consistently because it requires asking someone. Ask someone.

**3. People, not buildings.** Faces, in real moments. The single most common visual mistake in church social media is photographing the room instead of the people in it. Nobody has ever chosen a church because of a wide shot of the sanctuary.

**4. Answers to real questions.** "What do I wear?" "Do I have to talk to anyone?" "What happens if my kid cries?" These are the actual barriers to a first visit and almost nobody addresses them publicly. A 30-second video answering "what happens when you walk in" is more effective outreach than a month of verse graphics.

**5. Service and presence in the community.** What your church does on the other six days. Show it without narrating how humble you're being about it.

## What to stop

**Verse-on-a-gradient graphics.** Enormously common, essentially zero reach. They perform poorly because they're indistinguishable from ten thousand identical accounts. If you want to post scripture, film someone reading it.

**Cross-posting identical content everywhere.** Same video, fine. Same caption, aspect ratio, and hashtags across five platforms — that's how you end up with a TikTok with a Facebook caption on it. At minimum, adjust caption length and hook per platform.

**Posting in bursts.** Eight posts the week after a conference, then silence until Easter. Inconsistency is read as unreliability by both the algorithms and your congregation. Three posts a week you never miss beats seven you abandon.

**Making it about the institution.** "Join us as we continue our series in the book of..." is written for people who already know what a sermon series is. It's not wrong, it's just not reaching anyone new.

## The cadence question

Four posts a week is the target. Three is the floor. Here's the honest reason it's not higher: church media is done by volunteers, and any system that requires more than about two hours a week will be abandoned by month four.

## Measuring it without lying to yourself

Follower count is the least useful number available to you and the one leadership will ask about. Push back on it.

What actually indicates the account is working:

- **Saves and shares.** A share means someone put your content in front of their own network. That's the only real distribution you have. Ten shares is worth more than a thousand passive views.
- **Reach outside your following.** Instagram and Facebook both show you this. If 90% of your reach is existing followers, you're running a bulletin board.
- **"I saw your video" at the door.** Unmeasurable, and the most important signal there is. Ask first-time visitors how they heard about you and write it down.

Give it six months before drawing conclusions. Short-form content compounds slowly and then suddenly, and most churches quit at month three — right before the archive gets big enough to start working on its own.
