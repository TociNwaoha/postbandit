Most comparisons of AI clipping tools are written for podcasters and agencies. Church media teams have a genuinely different set of constraints, and the tool that wins for a podcaster with a budget is often the wrong pick for a volunteer running a church account on a Sunday afternoon.

This is a comparison written for the second group. Where a competitor is better for you, I've said so.

*Pricing verified against publicly available sources in August 2026. Plans change frequently — check the vendor's pricing page before you buy.*

## What churches actually need, in order

Before the table, it's worth naming the criteria — because the standard review criteria don't match this use case.

**1. Publishing to Facebook and Instagram.** This is the one that gets underweighted everywhere and matters most to churches. Your congregation is on Facebook. Your 20–40s are on Instagram Reels. A tool that produces beautiful clips and then makes you download and manually upload them five times has solved the wrong half of the problem — the manual publishing step is where volunteer teams actually lose their evening.

**2. Predictable monthly cost.** Churches budget annually and hate variable bills. Credit systems that can be blown through in a busy week are a real problem for a media budget approved by a committee in November.

**3. Long-source handling.** A sermon is 30–45 minutes; a full service is 90. Tools priced per minute of source video get expensive on this content faster than they do on a 20-minute podcast.

**4. Something a volunteer can learn in one sitting.** Turnover is constant. A tool that requires training is a tool that dies when one person moves away.

**5. Speaker tracking.** Only matters if your pastor moves around. If they preach from behind a pulpit, a static crop is fine and you can ignore this entirely.

## Where Opus Clip is genuinely better

I'm not going to pretend otherwise, so here it is plainly.

**It has a free plan and you should probably use it first.** <cite index="15-1">Sixty minutes of processing per month, watermarked</cite>. That's more than one sermon. If you have never clipped a sermon before, do it there before you spend anything anywhere, including here. You'll learn what you actually need.

**Years of clip-scoring data.** Opus Clip has been shipping since 2023 across an enormous volume of uploads. Their moment-detection generalizes across content types better than anything newer. On varied footage, that breadth shows.

**Speaker tracking is shipped and mature.** If your pastor walks the stage, this is a real, daily-felt advantage and PostBandit does not have it yet. It's on our roadmap; it is not in the product today, and I'm not going to imply otherwise.

**More caption languages.** If your church runs services in multiple languages, check this carefully — it's a decisive factor and Opus Clip is strong here.

## Where Vugola is genuinely better

**Cheapest entry point with publishing included**, and they publish to a wider list of platforms than we do — including Bluesky, which we don't support.

**They have a trial.** <cite index="3-1">Seven days, full plan credits, no watermark, card required up front.</cite> We don't offer one, which is a fair thing to hold against us.

**A real API and MCP server**, which matters if you're technical and want to script your workflow. Most church teams aren't and don't. If yours is, that's a genuine advantage.

## Where PostBandit fits

I'll be direct about what this is: a newer product, built by one person, aimed specifically at the sermon-to-social workflow rather than at creators generally.

**What that gets you:**

- **No processing cap.** Most tools meter source minutes. A church preaching weekly at 40 minutes burns 160 source minutes a month — more than Opus Clip's 150-minute Starter allowance. PostBandit meters storage instead (5 GiB), so you can clip as much source video as you want.
- **Facebook Pages publishing**, which matters more for churches than for almost any other segment and gets deprioritized by tools built for creators.
- **Built around this specific workflow.** Long single-speaker source, spoken-word content, a volunteer doing this on a Sunday afternoon.

**What you should know before choosing it:**

- **No free plan and no trial.** You're paying at least $9 to find out if it works for you. That's a real objection and I don't have a good answer for it yet.
- **5 GiB storage cap, and video is large.** A 40-minute 1080p sermon is roughly 1 GB, and storage counts source files, editor assets, and finished clips. You'll need to delete sources after clipping or you'll hit the ceiling. Uploads are blocked at 6 GiB.
- **There's a cheaper $9 plan, but it isn't right for churches.** Repurposer gives you 2 GiB and 3 connected accounts. One full-length sermon plus its clips can fill that in a single Sunday. If you're clipping weekly services, start at $18.
- **No speaker tracking yet.** If your pastor moves, use a static crop or a blurred-background layout for now. Or use Opus Clip Pro.
- **No Zoom or Boxcast integration.** You export the file and bring it in yourself, by upload or from a YouTube link.
- **It's new.** Fewer people have stress-tested it. That's a genuine risk and you should weight it.

## What I'd actually tell a church media team

**If you've never clipped a sermon:** start on Opus Clip's free plan. Do four weeks. Find out whether anyone on your team will actually keep doing this before you spend a dollar. Most churches discover the real constraint is volunteer time, not software.

**If your pastor moves around the stage a lot:** Opus Clip Pro at $29, or Vugola. Speaker tracking is worth paying for and we don't have it.

**If publishing to Facebook and Instagram is your actual bottleneck** — and for most churches it is — compare $18 here against $29 for Opus Clip Pro, and against $14 for Vugola if you're comfortable with a card-required trial and don't need Facebook Pages specifically.

**If you need a free tier:** we don't have one. Go elsewhere for now.

That's the honest version. If it turns out the free plan is enough for your church forever, that's a good outcome — you got sermon clips out into the world, which is the point.
