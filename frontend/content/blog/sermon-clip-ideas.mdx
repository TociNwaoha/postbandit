The hardest part of clipping sermons isn't the editing. It's sitting in front of a 40-minute transcript with no idea what's actually worth cutting.

Most teams default to whatever got the biggest reaction in the room. That's the wrong instinct, and it's why so many church clips underperform — applause lines are the *payoff* of a setup the viewer never saw. In the room, the setup happened twenty minutes ago and everyone remembers it. In the feed, the viewer arrived two seconds ago with no context and no goodwill.

Here's what actually travels.

## The 20

**1. The personal confession.** Any moment the pastor admits something costly about themselves. "I didn't want to write this sermon." "I was angry at God for about two years." This outperforms nearly everything else on the list, because sincerity is the one thing the feed can't fake at scale.

**2. The self-contained story.** A complete narrative with a beginning and an end, running 45–90 seconds. If the illustration has a setup, a turn, and a landing, it is already a short-form video.

**3. The reframe of a familiar verse.** Everybody has heard Jeremiah 29:11. A clip that says "this verse is not about your career" stops people, because it contradicts something they already believe.

**4. The hard question left hanging for a beat.** "What are you actually afraid of?" followed by three seconds of silence. Silence reads as confidence and holds retention better than filler.

**5. The correction of a common misreading.** "This is not what that word means in the original." Genuinely informative, and it gives the viewer something to repeat to someone else — which is what a share is.

**6. Direct address to a specific person.** "If you're the one who almost didn't come in this morning." Specificity feels like being seen. Vague address to "everyone" feels like being sold to.

**7. The one-sentence definition.** "Grace is getting what you didn't earn." Short, portable, screenshot-able. These work as static posts too.

**8. Anger at injustice.** A pastor genuinely angry about something worth being angry about — poverty, exploitation, hypocrisy in the church. Real conviction cuts through.

**9. The admission of not knowing.** "I don't have a good answer for that." Enormously disarming in a genre people assume is all certainty.

**10. Practical instruction.** "Here are three things to do this week when the anxiety hits." Saves are the most valuable signal on most platforms, and how-to content is what gets saved.

**11. The pushback against Christian cliché.** "Stop telling grieving people everything happens for a reason." People who've been hurt by the cliché will send this to someone.

**12. Scripture read well.** Not the whole chapter. Four to six verses, read slowly, over a clean shot. Understated and it performs steadily.

**13. The moment the pastor gets emotional.** Don't manufacture it, don't over-cut it, and check that they're comfortable with it going out. When it's real it's the most human thing on your feed.

**14. Historical or cultural context.** "In the first century, this would have been scandalous, and here's why." This is the sermon equivalent of a good explainer video.

**15. The unexpected concession.** "The critics of the church are right about this part." Intellectual honesty is rare enough in this genre that it registers as remarkable.

**16. Advice aimed at one demographic.** To parents. To people in their twenties. To someone considering leaving. Narrow beats broad — the narrow clip finds its people, the broad clip finds nobody.

**17. The analogy that lands.** Any moment an abstract idea gets made concrete. If it made you go "huh" while transcribing, it'll do the same in a feed.

**18. The invitation, on its own.** Not the whole altar call, but the thirty seconds of "you can come as you are." This is the closest thing to an ad you should post, and it works because it isn't structured like one.

**19. Follow-up on something the church already knows about.** A prayer request from a month ago that got answered. Continuity builds the sense that this is a real community and not a broadcast.

**20. The thing said off-script.** Look for where the delivery changes — where the cadence breaks and the pastor stops reading. Those moments are almost always more alive than the prepared material around them.

## The 5 that don't work

**1. The applause line, isolated.** Already covered, but it's the most common mistake so it's worth repeating. The reaction in the room was to twenty minutes of buildup. The clip contains none of it.

**2. Announcements.** Nobody outside your congregation cares, and the people inside it will see them somewhere else. Announcements belong in an email or a story, not a feed post.

**3. Anything under 15 seconds.** There's a persistent belief that shorter is always better. It isn't — a clip that ends before it establishes anything reads as filler and trains the algorithm that your account produces low-substance content.

**4. Worship music.** Two separate problems. Automated copyright detection will flag it on most platforms regardless of your streaming license, and even when it isn't flagged, worship footage rarely holds a scrolling viewer who has no relationship to the room. Details on the licensing side in [how to get permission to clip sermons](/blog/sermon-clipping-permissions).

**5. Anything requiring the previous ten minutes.** The test is simple and it's the only test that matters: read the clip out loud to someone who wasn't there. If you have to explain anything first, it isn't a clip.

## How to actually work through this

Don't watch the sermon looking for these. Read the transcript — it's about four times faster, and you'll catch things you'd miss listening because you're not being carried along by delivery.

Mark every candidate as you scan. Then apply one filter: **does the first sentence make someone want the second one?** That question kills about half your candidates and it's a better predictor than anything else.

From a 40-minute sermon you should end up with roughly eight usable clips. Publish four this week. Hold four for next month, when the sermon is out of everyone's memory and the clip will feel new again — your archive is a genuine asset and most churches never touch it.
